Ride-hailing platforms like Uber, Ola, and Lyft look deceptively simple from the outside.

A rider opens the app, chooses a destination, sees a price, books a cab, gets matched with a driver, and watches the driver approach in real time.

Behind that seemingly straightforward flow is a system dealing with:

- millions of moving entities,
- continuously changing GPS coordinates,
- geospatial search,
- dynamic pricing,
- real-time matching,
- graph-based routing,
- ETA prediction,
- concurrency,
- distributed state,
- and extremely latency-sensitive user interactions.

This case study walks through how we can design such a system from first principles.

---

## 1. Requirements

We will focus on the following major capabilities:

1. Estimate the price of a ride.
2. Apply surge pricing based on local demand and supply.
3. Find nearby drivers.
4. Match a rider with an appropriate driver.
5. Continuously process driver location updates.
6. Calculate driver pickup ETA.
7. Calculate trip ETA.
8. Support drivers who may become available shortly.
9. Book the ride safely without double-assigning drivers.

We will intentionally keep features such as payments, ratings, chat, customer support, promotions, and fraud detection outside the initial scope.

---

## 2. The Basic Ride Flow

At the highest level:

```text
Rider
  |
  | source + destination
  v
Ride Service
  |
  +----> Pricing Service
  |
  +----> Matching Service
  |
  +----> ETA / Routing Service
  |
  v
Driver Assigned
```

The difficult part is not creating a ride row in a database.

The difficult part is answering questions such as:

> Which driver can reach this rider fastest?

> What should this trip cost right now?

> How do I find drivers near a coordinate among millions of moving drivers?

> What if two riders try to reserve the same driver?

> What if the best driver is currently completing another trip?

Those are the real system-design problems.

## 3. Static Ride Pricing

Let's begin with a simplified fare model.

Suppose:

- $S$ = source
- $D$ = destination

A basic ride price may be modeled as:

$$
\text{Price} = \text{Distance}(S,D) \times W_d + \text{Time}(S,D) \times W_t
$$

where:

- $W_d$ = price per unit distance
- $W_t$ = price per unit time

Why use both distance and time?

Because two 10 km journeys are not necessarily equivalent.

A 10 km highway trip might take 12 minutes.

A 10 km city-centre trip during rush hour might take 45 minutes.

The latter occupies driver capacity for much longer.

Therefore pricing usually incorporates both.

A more realistic pricing equation may look like:

$$
\text{Fare} = \text{BaseFare} + \text{DistanceRate} \times \text{Distance} + \text{TimeRate} \times \text{Duration} + \text{Fees} + \text{Surge} - \text{Discounts}
$$

---

## 4. Why Use a Pricing Rule Engine?

A naive implementation could contain pricing conditions directly in the application:

```java
if (city.equals("Hyderabad")) {
    if (vehicleType.equals("Sedan")) {
        if (hour >= 8 && hour <= 11) {
            distanceRate = 16;
        }
    }
}
```

This becomes unmaintainable extremely quickly.

Pricing can depend on:

- city,
- cab category,
- airport pickup,
- airport drop-off,
- time of day,
- weekday/weekend,
- taxes,
- tolls,
- platform fee,
- driver incentives,
- user membership,
- promotions,
- regulatory rules.

Pricing rules also change much more frequently than core application code.

So instead, we can separate them into a **Rule Engine**.

```text
Pricing Service
      |
      v
 Rule Engine
      |
      v
Pricing Configuration
```

A rule might conceptually say:

```text
IF city = Hyderabad
AND cabType = Sedan
AND hour BETWEEN 8 AND 11

THEN
distanceRate = ₹16/km
timeRate = ₹2/min
```

The benefit is operational flexibility.

Changing a pricing rule no longer requires redeploying the entire booking system.

---

## 5. Caching Pricing Rules

Pricing is highly latency-sensitive.

Users expect fare estimates almost immediately after entering their destination.

Therefore frequently used pricing rules can be cached.

```text
Pricing Service
      |
      v
 Rule Cache
      |
   cache miss
      |
      v
 Pricing DB
```

Redis or even service-local caches may be used depending on consistency requirements.

Notice the distinction:

**Cache**

> Quickly retrieves configuration.

**Rule Engine**

> Determines which configuration applies.

These solve different problems.

## 6. Geographic Pricing Regions

Pricing and surge calculations cannot realistically operate on arbitrary latitude-longitude values independently.

We need to divide the world into geographical regions.

Conceptually:

```text
+----+----+----+----+
| A1 | A2 | A3 | A4 |
+----+----+----+----+
| B1 | B2 | B3 | B4 |
+----+----+----+----+
| C1 | C2 | C3 | C4 |
+----+----+----+----+
```

Each coordinate maps into a geographical cell.

For example:

```text
17.4435, 78.3772
        ↓
     Cell B3
```

Production systems can use spatial partitioning schemes such as:

- Geohash,
- Google S2,
- Uber H3.

The exact technology matters less than understanding the underlying principle:

> Convert arbitrary coordinates into discrete spatial regions that can be indexed efficiently.

---

## 7. Choosing Region Granularity

The cell size introduces an important trade-off.

If cells are too large:

```text
large region
  |
  +-- area with massive demand
  +-- area with almost no demand
```

Averages hide local conditions.

If cells are too small:

```text
Cell A:
3 riders
1 driver

Cell B:
1 rider
3 drivers
```

Moving 100 metres might produce dramatically different prices.

Small cells also create:

- more counters,
- greater memory usage,
- more updates,
- noisier demand estimates.

Therefore:

$$
\text{Spatial Accuracy} \longleftrightarrow \text{Stability} + \text{Computational Cost}
$$

The optimal resolution can even vary by region.

Downtown Hyderabad may warrant smaller cells than rural outskirts.

## 8. Surge Pricing

Static pricing alone is insufficient.

Suppose a region contains:

```text
1,000 ride requests
200 available drivers
```

Demand vastly exceeds supply.

The platform may increase prices.

At its simplest:

$$
\text{SurgeMultiplier} = f\left(\frac{\text{Demand}}{\text{Supply}}\right)
$$

And:

$$
\text{FinalFare} = \text{StaticFare} \times \text{SurgeMultiplier}
$$

The objective is not only higher revenue.

Surge pricing acts as a marketplace-balancing mechanism.

Higher prices can:

1. reduce rider demand,
2. attract drivers toward the high-demand region.

So:

```text
Demand >> Supply
       |
       v
Higher Price
    /       \
   v         v
Demand ↓   Supply ↑
```

That helps restore equilibrium.

## 9. Static vs Dynamic Pricing

A useful mental model is:

```text
Static Pricing
---------------
distance
time
cab category
base fare
city rules

        +

Dynamic Pricing
---------------
demand
supply
local conditions
real-time marketplace state

        =

Final Quote
```

These should generally be treated as related but distinct subsystems.

## 10. Quote Stability

There is a subtle consistency issue.

Suppose the app shows:

```text
₹420
```

The rider waits 10 seconds and presses **Book**.

Meanwhile surge changes and the price becomes:

```text
₹510
```

We probably should not silently charge ₹510.

A useful solution is issuing a short-lived **quote**.

```json
{
  "quoteId": "q_92183",
  "fare": 420,
  "expiresAt": "..."
}
```

The booking request then references the quote.

```text
GET PRICE
   ↓
Quote ID
   ↓
BOOK RIDE using Quote ID
```

This provides a bounded period of price consistency.

---

## 11. Ride Matching

Now comes one of the core problems.

A rider requests a cab from pickup location $P$.

A naive design might say:

> Find the nearest driver.

That is not enough.

The closest driver geographically may not be the fastest driver to reach the passenger.

Consider:

```text
Driver A
700m away
ETA = 8 min

Driver B
1.5km away
ETA = 4 min
```

Driver B is clearly better.

Why?

Because roads have:

- one-way restrictions,
- traffic,
- flyovers,
- dividers,
- prohibited turns,
- congestion,
- different road speeds.

So matching must optimize primarily around:

$$
\text{ETA}(\text{driver}, \text{pickup})
$$

rather than:

$$
\text{StraightLineDistance}(\text{driver}, \text{pickup})
$$

---

## 12. Two-Stage Driver Search

However, computing an accurate road ETA for every active driver would be absurdly expensive.

Imagine:

```text
100,000 active drivers in a city
```

We cannot perform 100,000 routing computations for every rider.

Instead, use a **coarse-to-fine search**.

```text
All Drivers
     |
     v
Geospatial Filter
     |
     v
Nearby Drivers
     |
     v
Accurate ETA Calculation
     |
     v
Best Candidates
```

For example:

```text
100,000 drivers
      ↓
200 geographically nearby
      ↓
30 likely candidates
      ↓
10 accurate ETA calculations
      ↓
top candidates
```

This pattern is extremely common in large-scale systems.

First use a cheap approximation.

Then run expensive calculations on a small candidate set.

---

## 13. Expanding Radius Search

Suppose we initially search a 1 km area around the rider.

```text
Rider
  ●
 (1 km radius)
```

We find five eligible drivers.

All five reject the ride.

Instead of failing immediately, expand the search.

```text
1 km
 ↓
2 km
 ↓
3 km
 ↓
5 km
```

This is essentially an **expanding-ring search**.

Conceptually:

```text
Search nearby region
        |
        v
Find candidates
        |
        v
Send ride requests
        |
      Accepted?
      /       \
    yes       no
     |         |
 Assign    expand region
```

![Two-Stage Driver Filtering and Expanding Search Radius Pipeline](/images/blog/uber-driver-matching-radius.jpg)

## 14. Search Radius Trade-Off

Searching too wide immediately:

- wastes routing computation,
- notifies unnecessary drivers,
- returns potentially poor pickup ETAs.

Searching too narrowly:

- requires repeated retries,
- increases booking latency.

A smarter system can choose the initial search radius dynamically based on supply density.

Dense city centre:

```text
small initial region
```

Suburban area:

```text
larger initial region
```

---

## 15. Don't Dispatch to Every Driver

Suppose 80 drivers satisfy our matching criteria.

Sending the request to all 80 is undesirable.

We could instead use batches.

```text
Top 3
  ↓
wait briefly
  ↓
No acceptance
  ↓
Next 5
```

There are trade-offs.

### Sequential dispatch

Advantages:

- fewer duplicate acceptances,
- fewer unnecessary notifications.

Disadvantages:

- slower matching.

### Parallel dispatch

Advantages:

- lower rider latency.

Disadvantages:

- race conditions,
- more driver spam,
- wasted dispatches.

Production systems usually use some hybrid strategy.

---

## 16. Driver Reservation and Concurrency

Consider this situation:

```text
Rider A ----\
             > Driver X
Rider B ----/
```

Two matching servers simultaneously observe:

```text
Driver X = AVAILABLE
```

Both try to assign the driver.

Without coordination:

```text
Ride A → Driver X
Ride B → Driver X
```

We have a serious correctness bug.

Driver allocation therefore needs an atomic state transition.

For example:

```text
AVAILABLE
    ↓
RESERVED
    ↓
ASSIGNED
```

Conceptually:

```java
compareAndSet(
    driverId,
    AVAILABLE,
    RESERVED_FOR_RIDE_123
);
```

Only one request succeeds.

This leads to a very useful distributed-systems distinction.

### Driver location

Slightly stale values are acceptable.

### Driver assignment

Incorrect concurrent state is not acceptable.

Therefore:

```text
GPS location
→ eventual consistency acceptable

Driver reservation
→ strong consistency required
```

This is an excellent CAP/consistency discussion point in interviews.

## 17. Driver Location Updates

Ride matching depends heavily on fresh driver positions.

Drivers continuously send:

```json
{
  "driverId": 123,
  "latitude": 17.44,
  "longitude": 78.38,
  "timestamp": "...",
  "speed": 34,
  "heading": 125,
  "availability": "AVAILABLE"
}
```

Suppose:

```text
1,000,000 online drivers
```

and each sends one update every four seconds.

Then the system receives:

$$
\frac{1,000,000}{4} = 250,000
$$

location updates per second.

This is a huge write workload.

A traditional relational database should not be the direct destination of every update.

A better architecture is:

```text
Driver Apps
     |
     v
Location Ingestion Service
     |
     v
Streaming Platform
     |
     +----> Live Geo Index
     |
     +----> Matching System
     |
     +----> Historical Storage
```

Kafka or an equivalent durable event stream fits naturally here.

## 18. The Live Geo Index

The matching system needs to answer:

> Give me available drivers near coordinate X.

That is fundamentally a geospatial query.

We can map drivers into geographic cells.

```text
Driver location update
        |
        v
Coordinate → Cell ID
        |
        v
Cell → set of active drivers
```

Conceptually:

```text
H3 Cell 8a2a...
-----------------
Driver 12
Driver 45
Driver 91
Driver 108
```

When the rider requests a ride:

```text
Pickup
  ↓
Cell ID
  ↓
Current cell + neighbor cells
  ↓
Candidate drivers
```

This is much faster than scanning all drivers.

## 19. Why ETA Requires a Road Graph

Geospatial indexing tells us:

> Which drivers are nearby?

It does **not** tell us:

> Which driver can reach the rider fastest?

For that, we need the road network.

Road networks naturally form graphs.

```text
Intersections → Nodes
Roads         → Edges
Travel cost   → Edge weights
```

Example:

```text
A ----5---- B ----3---- C
|           |
10          2
|           |
D ----4---- E
```

If the edge weights represent expected travel time:

$$
w(u,v) = \text{ExpectedTravelTime}(u,v)
$$

then ETA becomes a shortest-path problem.

---

## 20. ETA as Shortest Path

For source $S$ and destination $D$:

$$
\text{ETA}(S,D) = \min_{P:S\rightarrow D} \sum_{e \in P} w(e)
$$

We want the path whose sum of road-segment travel times is smallest.

Importantly:

> shortest-time route ≠ shortest-distance route.

A 5 km highway route may outperform a congested 3 km route.

![Road Graph Network and Shortest-Path ETA Routing via A* Search](/images/blog/uber-road-graph-a-star-routing.jpg)

---

## 21. Dynamic Edge Weights

Road topology changes relatively slowly.

Traffic changes constantly.

Therefore:

```text
Road topology
→ mostly static

Edge weights
→ dynamic
```

A road segment may take:

```text
2 AM  → 30 sec
9 AM  → 140 sec
6 PM  → 220 sec
```

So the weight is really:

$$
w(e,t)
$$

where $t$ represents time.

The estimate may depend on:

$$
w = f(\text{distance}, \text{historical traffic}, \text{live traffic}, \text{speed limits}, \text{road type}, \text{incidents}, \text{weather})
$$

This means we should separate:

```text
Base Road Graph
      +
Dynamic Traffic Weights
      ↓
Routing Engine
```

---

## 22. Dijkstra's Algorithm

The classic shortest-path algorithm is Dijkstra.

It explores nodes according to the cheapest accumulated cost from the source:

$$
g(n)
$$

For a huge road network, however, Dijkstra may explore too much of the graph.

If we are routing inside a graph with millions of nodes, doing an unrestricted search for every ETA request becomes expensive.

---

## 23. A* Search

A* improves the search by using knowledge of the destination.

Instead of considering only:

$$
g(n)
$$

it considers:

$$
f(n) = g(n) + h(n)
$$

where:

- $g(n)$ = actual cost from source to $n$
- $h(n)$ = estimated remaining cost from $n$ to destination.

Conceptually:

```text
source
  |
known cost
  |
 node
  |
estimated remaining cost
  |
destination
```

The routing engine prioritizes nodes with the smallest:

$$
g(n) + h(n)
$$

So A* tends to search toward the destination rather than expanding equally in every direction.

---

## 24. The Heuristic

A simple heuristic could be:

$$
h(n) = \frac{\text{StraightLineDistance}(n,D)}{\text{MaximumTravelSpeed}}
$$

For optimality, the heuristic should not overestimate the true remaining cost.

That property is known as **admissibility**.

$$
h(n) \le \text{ActualCost}(n,D)
$$

With an admissible heuristic, A* retains shortest-path optimality.

A useful interview memory hook:

> A* is essentially Dijkstra plus a destination-aware heuristic.

---

## 25. A* Does Not Predict Traffic

This distinction matters.

A* solves:

> Given edge costs, what route minimizes total cost?

It does **not** magically determine what those edge costs should be.

ETA therefore involves two separate problems.

### Travel-time prediction

Estimate how long each road segment will take.

### Route optimization

Find the lowest-cost combination of road segments.

Conceptually:

```text
Traffic Model
     |
     v
Edge Weights
     |
     v
Routing Algorithm
     |
     v
Route + ETA
```

This separation is a much stronger way to explain ETA in an interview.

---

## 26. GPS Map Matching

A driver's GPS coordinate will rarely correspond exactly to a graph node.

Suppose:

```text
17.451928, 78.381521
```

The routing system needs to determine the appropriate road segment.

This process is called **map matching**.

```text
Raw GPS
   ↓
Map Matching
   ↓
Road Segment / Graph Position
   ↓
Routing
```

Without map matching, noisy GPS could place a vehicle:

- inside a building,
- on the wrong side of a divided road,
- on a parallel street.

---

## 27. Multiple ETA Types

Ride-hailing systems require several ETAs.

### Driver pickup ETA

$$
\text{ETA}(\text{driver}, \text{pickup})
$$

Used for matching.

### Trip ETA

$$
\text{ETA}(\text{pickup}, \text{destination})
$$

Used for:

- pricing,
- rider expectations,
- trip planning.

### Busy-driver future ETA

We will shortly introduce:

$$
\text{ETA}(\text{driver}, \text{currentDropoff}) + \text{ETA}(\text{currentDropoff}, \text{newPickup})
$$

Used for predicting when an occupied driver may become useful again.

---

## 28. Avoid Recomputing Everything Constantly

Suppose one million rides are active and we recalculate routes every three seconds.

That produces:

$$
333,333
$$

routing computations per second.

Potentially extremely expensive.

We can optimize through:

- route caching,
- approximate ETA decrementing,
- recalculation only after meaningful route deviation,
- recomputation when traffic changes materially,
- batching,
- shared route-segment calculations.

For example:

```text
Previous ETA = 8m00s

3 seconds pass
Driver stays on route

Approx ETA ≈ 7m57s
```

We do not necessarily need another full graph search.

---

## 29. Dedicated Routing Infrastructure

It may be tempting to say:

> Roads are a graph, so let's put them in Neo4j.

Not necessarily.

General-purpose graph databases are not automatically ideal for ultra-low-latency routing.

A routing server can instead maintain optimized in-memory representations:

```text
adjacencyList[nodeId]
    ↓
[
    neighbor + cost,
    neighbor + cost,
    neighbor + cost
]
```

The road network may be persisted elsewhere and loaded into specialized routing workers.

Thus:

```text
Persistent Graph Data
        |
        v
Routing Cluster
(in-memory optimized graph)
```

---

## 30. Geographic Routing Partitions

A global road graph is enormous.

We don't need every server to load every road on Earth.

Routing infrastructure may be geographically partitioned.

```text
India
 ├── Hyderabad
 ├── Bangalore
 ├── Delhi
 └── Mumbai
```

Potentially further divided into regional partitions.

Trade-off:

### Smaller partitions

Pros:

- less memory,
- strong locality.

Cons:

- cross-partition routing complexity.

### Larger partitions

Pros:

- easier path computation.

Cons:

- larger memory footprint.

---

## 31. Graceful Degradation

What happens if the routing service temporarily fails?

Reject every ride?

That would be terrible availability.

Instead, degrade.

```text
Live Routing unavailable
        |
        v
Historical ETA / cached route
        |
        v
Approximate result
```

Similarly:

```text
Live traffic unavailable
        ↓
historical traffic profile
```

Worst-case fallback might approximate based on:

$$
\text{StraightLineDistance} + \text{HistoricalAverageSpeed}
$$

The estimate becomes less accurate, but the service remains usable.

This is a classic distributed-system principle:

> Degraded functionality is often preferable to total failure.

---

## 32. Matching Drivers Who Are Still Busy

A particularly interesting optimization is to not treat availability as purely binary.

Suppose a driver is currently carrying another passenger but will finish the trip in four minutes.

The new rider is three minutes away from that driver's drop-off point.

Then:

$$
4 + 3 = 7\text{ minutes}
$$

If the rider's maximum wait threshold is 10 minutes, that driver may already be a viable candidate.

The formula becomes:

$$
\text{ETA}(\text{DriverCurrentLocation}, \text{CurrentTripDestination}) + \text{ETA}(\text{CurrentTripDestination}, \text{NewPickup}) \leq \text{Threshold}
$$

---

## 33. Effective Pickup ETA

We can unify idle and busy drivers using one abstraction:

$$
\text{EffectivePickupETA}(\text{driver}, \text{ride})
$$

For an idle driver:

$$
\text{EffectivePickupETA} = \text{ETA}(\text{driver}, \text{pickup})
$$

For a busy driver:

$$
\text{EffectivePickupETA} = \text{ETA}(\text{driver}, \text{currentDropoff}) + \text{ETA}(\text{currentDropoff}, \text{newPickup})
$$

Now every driver candidate can be compared through the same metric.

## 34. Driver Availability Is Not Binary

Instead of thinking only:

```text
OFFLINE
AVAILABLE
BUSY
```

we can model:

```text
OFFLINE
AVAILABLE
RESERVED
ON_TRIP
AVAILABLE_SOON
```

`AVAILABLE_SOON` could simply be a dynamically derived state.

This increases marketplace utilization.

Otherwise we wait until:

```text
Trip ends
  ↓
Driver state updated
  ↓
Matching notices
  ↓
Driver becomes candidate
```

That creates unnecessary idle time.

## 35. Future Reservation Risk

Pre-matching introduces uncertainty.

Suppose a driver is expected to finish their current ride in eight minutes.

But:

- traffic worsens,
- rider changes destination,
- driver takes a wrong turn,
- current passenger adds a stop,
- driver goes offline after drop-off.

So future matches should be treated carefully.

Possible strategy:

```text
ON_TRIP
   ↓
TENTATIVELY_MATCHED
   ↓
revalidation
   ↓
ASSIGNED
```

Future reservations can have:

- TTL,
- periodic revalidation,
- cancellation paths.

---

## 36. ETA Uncertainty

Two drivers may both show:

```text
ETA = 5 min
```

But their confidence levels differ.

Driver A:

```text
already idle
5 minutes away
```

Driver B:

```text
still finishing a trip
expected pickup ETA = 5 minutes
```

Driver B's ETA has more uncertainty.

Therefore ranking could consider something like:

$$
\text{Score} = w_1 \times \text{PickupETA} + w_2 \times \text{ETAUncertainty} + w_3 \times \text{AcceptanceProbability} + w_4 \times \text{CancellationRisk}
$$

This is where the matching problem begins evolving from a deterministic algorithm into a marketplace optimization problem.

---

## 37. Matching Is a Ranking Problem

The absolute nearest driver may not always be optimal.

A real matching score could consider:

- pickup ETA,
- driver acceptance probability,
- rider cancellation probability,
- cab type,
- current driver idle time,
- future supply-demand conditions,
- destination attractiveness,
- driver fairness,
- driver earnings,
- utilization.

Conceptually:

$$
\text{Score}(\text{driver}, \text{ride}) = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \dots + w_n x_n
$$

and choose the candidate that minimizes cost or maximizes marketplace utility.

This can evolve into:

- regression models,
- ranking models,
- optimization algorithms,
- reinforcement learning,
- operations research techniques.

A reasonable architecture now looks like:

## 41. Ride State Machine

Ride lifecycle should be modeled explicitly.

For example:

```text
REQUESTED
    |
    v
SEARCHING
    |
    v
DRIVER_RESERVED
    |
    v
DRIVER_ACCEPTED
    |
    v
DRIVER_ARRIVING
    |
    v
DRIVER_ARRIVED
    |
    v
IN_PROGRESS
    |
    v
COMPLETED
```

Alternative terminal states:

```text
CANCELLED_BY_RIDER
CANCELLED_BY_DRIVER
MATCH_FAILED
EXPIRED
```

Explicit state machines are valuable because illegal transitions can then be prevented.

For example:

```text
COMPLETED → DRIVER_ACCEPTED
```

should obviously never occur.

## 43. Scaling Strategy

Different components experience very different workloads.

This is precisely why separating services makes sense.

### Location ingestion

Characteristics:

- massive write rate,
- frequent updates,
- stale data quickly becomes useless.

Optimize for:

- throughput,
- partitioning,
- stream processing.

### Pricing

Characteristics:

- read-heavy,
- low latency,
- rules reused frequently.

Optimize using:

- caching,
- rule preloading,
- stateless horizontal scaling.

### Matching

Characteristics:

- compute intensive,
- latency sensitive,
- high concurrency.

Optimize using:

- geographical partitioning,
- local candidate sets,
- parallel computation.

### Routing

Characteristics:

- CPU/memory intensive,
- repeated queries,
- graph-heavy computation.

Optimize using:

- in-memory graph partitions,
- cached routes,
- hierarchical routing.

## 44. Geographic Sharding

Ride-hailing systems have a useful natural sharding dimension:

> geography.

A rider in Hyderabad generally does not need to match with a driver in Delhi.

So:

```text
India
 |
 +-- Hyderabad Matching Cluster
 |
 +-- Bangalore Matching Cluster
 |
 +-- Delhi Matching Cluster
```

We can potentially subdivide very large cities further.

This provides:

- reduced search space,
- lower latency,
- better cache locality,
- easier horizontal scaling.

## 45. Consistency Choices

Not every piece of information needs strong consistency.

### Location

Eventually consistent.

A 2-second-old location is usually acceptable.

### Surge counters

Eventually or near-real-time consistent.

Small temporary differences between adjacent servers are tolerable.

### Pricing rules

Generally strongly versioned but aggressively cached.

### Driver reservation

Strong consistency.

A driver must not be assigned to two rides.

### Ride state

Strong consistency around critical transitions.

This is a much stronger way to discuss CAP than simply saying:

> “Uber needs availability so we'll choose AP.”

Different subproblems need different consistency guarantees.

## 46. Major Failure Modes

A strong system-design answer should explicitly discuss failure.

### Location updates stop arriving

Possible reasons:

- poor network,
- phone background restrictions,
- GPS failure.

Response:

- mark location increasingly stale,
- lower matching priority,
- eventually mark driver temporarily unavailable.

---

### Driver accepts after reservation expired

Use:

- reservation tokens,
- version numbers,
- TTL.

Reject stale acceptance.

---

### Two drivers accept the same ride

Ride reservation must also be atomic.

Only one driver transitions the ride from:

```text
SEARCHING
```

to:

```text
DRIVER_ASSIGNED
```

Others receive failure/withdrawal.

---

### Matching service crashes midway

Persist sufficient ride state so another worker can resume.

Use idempotent operations.

---

### Routing service unavailable

Fallback to:

- cached ETA,
- historical estimate,
- coarse distance-based approximation.

---

### Kafka/location pipeline lag

Monitor event lag.

If lag becomes too high, location data is effectively stale even if the system is technically processing it.

## 48. Important Bottlenecks

The most likely bottlenecks are not ordinary database reads.

They are:

### 1. Location ingestion

Potentially hundreds of thousands or millions of events per second.

### 2. Geospatial search

Finding nearby drivers continuously.

### 3. ETA computation

Graph search is computationally expensive.

### 4. Dispatch fan-out

One ride may involve several driver notifications.

### 5. Hot regions

Airports, stadiums, railway stations, concerts, and rush-hour business districts can create extremely skewed workloads.

That last one is particularly important.

Geographic partitioning does not guarantee even load.

A single H3 cell around an airport may become much hotter than surrounding cells.

## 49. Handling Hot Geographic Partitions

Suppose Hyderabad Airport suddenly receives thousands of ride requests.

One geographic shard may become overloaded.

Possible strategies include:

- subdividing hot cells,
- replicating read-heavy geo indexes,
- dynamic worker assignment,
- hashing within a region,
- adaptive partitioning.

This is an example of why:

> “Shard by geography”

is only the beginning of the answer.

You must also consider **skew**.

## 50. Cost Considerations

The most expensive parts are likely:

- high-frequency location ingestion,
- routing compute,
- persistent connections,
- map/traffic data,
- stream infrastructure,
- large in-memory geospatial indexes.

Cost can be reduced by avoiding unnecessary precision.

For example, an idle driver's position might need frequent updates.

An offline driver's position does not.

Similarly:

```text
Driver moving quickly
→ frequent updates

Driver parked for 15 min
→ reduce frequency
```

Adaptive update frequency can substantially reduce:

- mobile battery use,
- bandwidth,
- backend traffic.

## 51. The Most Important Design Pattern Here

This entire system repeatedly uses the same pattern:

> Cheap approximation first, expensive precision later.

For driver matching:

```text
Geo distance
   ↓
candidate shortlist
   ↓
accurate ETA
```

For routing:

```text
heuristic
   ↓
guided graph exploration
```

For availability:

```text
cheap future ETA estimation
   ↓
accurate evaluation for top candidates
```

At large scale, performing the most accurate computation on everything is rarely affordable.

## 52. Final Interview Design

If I had to explain the complete design quickly in an interview, I would say:

> Rider requests first go through pricing, where route distance/time are combined with cached rule configuration and region-specific surge data to produce a short-lived quote.
>
> Driver applications continuously publish GPS updates into a high-throughput location ingestion pipeline. Those updates maintain an in-memory/geospatial index partitioned by spatial cells.
>
> When a rider books, the matching service converts the pickup point into a geo cell, retrieves nearby drivers, and performs a coarse-to-fine candidate search. Cheap spatial distance filters candidates first, after which the routing service computes accurate road ETAs for a shortlist.
>
> Routing operates over a weighted road graph where intersections are nodes, road segments are edges, and dynamic travel-time predictions become edge weights. A destination-aware shortest-path algorithm such as A* can compute the best route efficiently.
>
> Matching ranks candidates by pickup ETA and additional marketplace factors. Drivers currently completing rides may also be considered using current-trip completion ETA plus travel time from the current drop-off to the new pickup.
>
> Ride requests are dispatched to a small batch of drivers. Driver reservation uses a strongly consistent atomic state transition so the same driver cannot be assigned to multiple riders. If nobody accepts, the geographic search expands.
>
> Location, surge data, and similar high-frequency signals can tolerate eventual consistency, while driver reservation and critical ride-state transitions require strong consistency.

That is the design in a nutshell.

Before a system-design interview, remember this chain:

```text
PRICE
↓

↓

↓
surge by region


LOCATION
↓
driver GPS updates
↓
stream
↓
geo index


MATCHING
↓
geo candidate lookup
↓
coarse filter
↓
ETA shortlist
↓
rank
↓
dispatch
↓
atomic reservation


ETA
↓
road graph
↓
nodes + edges + dynamic weights
↓
A*
↓
traffic-aware shortest-time path


ADVANCED MATCHING
↓
idle drivers
+
busy drivers finishing soon
↓
effective pickup ETA
↓
future marketplace optimization
```

And remember these four distinctions especially:

**Geospatial proximity ≠ ETA**

Geospatial indexes find nearby entities. Routing tells you how quickly they are actually reachable.

**A* ≠ ETA prediction**

Traffic models predict edge weights. A* searches for the optimal route using those weights.

**Location consistency ≠ booking consistency**

Location can be slightly stale. Driver assignment must not be inconsistent.

**Driver availability ≠ binary state**

A driver who is busy now may be the best driver eight minutes from now.

## Conclusion

A ride aggregator is fundamentally a **real-time geospatial marketplace**.

Its hardest problems are not conventional CRUD operations. They arise from continuously changing supply and demand.

At its core, the system combines:

- geospatial indexing to find nearby drivers,
- graph algorithms to calculate travel time,
- streaming infrastructure to ingest location updates,
- dynamic pricing to balance the marketplace,
- ranking algorithms to choose candidates,
- and strongly consistent state transitions to safely allocate scarce resources.

The most useful mental model is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Location} \longrightarrow \text{Candidates} \longrightarrow \text{ETA} \longrightarrow \text{Ranking} \longrightarrow \text{Reservation}}
$$

Once you understand that pipeline, most of the architecture around Uber-style systems becomes much easier to reason about.

And that is the bigger system-design lesson here: **don't begin with technologies. Begin by identifying which computations must be approximate, which must be real-time, which require strong correctness, and which can tolerate stale data. The architecture follows from those constraints.**
